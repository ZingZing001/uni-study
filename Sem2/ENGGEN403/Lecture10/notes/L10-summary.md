# ⚡ ENGGEN403 L10 — one-page summary

> ⭐ **"You can't just pull one lever marked 'government'. You have to know which part of which ministry is advising which minister to advocate in cabinet to make which decision — and how those decisions might impact locally, and how local government might respond."**

## 🗺 Central vs local

**Central** makes laws for the whole country. **Local** makes local decisions (amenities, environment). **Many systems problems cut across both** — central sets policy, local **enacts and resources** it (does the recycling, resources the food banks).

⚠ **The interface is a boundary condition** — Wellington saving money vs councils investing in libraries and parks. Navigate with relationships; find what's good for both.

Structures: **Regional Councils, Territorial Authorities, Unitary Authorities** — *"don't worry about the details"*. **Auckland = Unitary Authority**, elected **Mayor chairs the Council**, **21 local boards** (~150 members) report up. The muddle is defensible: *"one size doesn't fit all… Auckland is very different to Kaipara or Nelson."*

## 🏙 Auckland

Council manages **emergency management, planning and consents, pools, beaches and parks, public spaces, waste and water quality** — the city, suburbs, rural areas, streams and coasts. Central government's role is *"to make decisions and create laws for all of our motu."*

⭐ **A mayor cannot simply decide.** 20 other elected councillors, **no party structure**. *"Wayne's job is to find a majority… it's perfectly possible that a mayor disagrees with all the other councillors and can't get anything done."*

**The swimming pool** needed: advice from policy officials → half the councillors → the local board → the budget. **Four gates for one pool.**

**Council organisation ≠ governing body.** Staff stay when mayors change — institutional knowledge. **Council-controlled organisations** are controlled by but not part of council, to keep things at arm's length (Auckland Transport, until recently).

## 👔 Elected vs appointed ⭐

Two categories: **politicians (elected MPs)** and **public servants**. In NZ **public servants are not political appointments — they keep their job whoever wins**. They **provide independent advice** and then **implement**.

**Jacinda and Ashley** looked like one unit during COVID; Ardern was elected, Bloomfield appointed. *"Ashley was director-general for Jacinda, but also for Chris Hipkins and also for Chris Luxon."* Directors-general can serve **20 years across many governments** → institutional knowledge, independent advice, *"the politics of the day doesn't completely control the policy."*

⚠ Not absolute — governments *"tend to appoint someone more aligned with their policy position"* — but NZ scores very highly internationally on public service independence.

> ⭐ **"When you're giving technical advice to influence policy, you are generally aiming at public servants like Ashley, not at political appointments like Jacinda."**

**Slide 8 repeats L3 verbatim:** science is never the only advice · good at defining the problem · good at identifying options · struggles with definitive timely answers · politicians decide in defined timeframes · **policy makers bridge the divide** · presenting the facts rarely changed anyone's mind · science debate ≠ proxy for values debate.

## 🏢 Finding the policy agenda

**~41 departments** — *"do not quote me on that number"*, they merge and shrink. Cabinet is *"the ultimate decision maker"*; Parliament clears the House.

| **Manifesto commitments and coalition agreements** | Promises, then the negotiated version — *"if you are lucky, they will publish those agreements"* |
| **100-day plans** | *"Even more useful… but they stopped doing that"* |
| ⭐ **Briefings for Incoming Ministers (BIMs)** | Departments' own view: *"these are the things I would have on my radar"* — institutional knowledge, immune to political winds |
| **Ministerial priorities / portfolio work programme** | *"They might announce… or they might not"* |
| **Flexibility for emerging issues and crises** | *"They have to be nimble"* |

**MMP means coalitions** — a single-party majority has happened once in recent memory.

## 🔗 The advice chain

**Agencies (policy advice) → Ministers → Cabinet → Parliament → Agencies (implementation/evaluation).**

> *"This is the process for change. And this is the one you need to influence if you need to change the system."*

**Failure modes:** advice not taken · ⭐ **ministers convinced but can't convince cabinet colleagues** — *"ministers compete for resource and compete for priority around the cabinet table"* (three parties in one cabinet) · a coalition party refusing to support a cabinet decision in Parliament (**India FTA — NZ First withdrew, Labour stepped in**) · then funding.

Cabinet is private: *"we don't know, those meetings are private."*

## 💰 The budget cycle

| **Strategic phase** | **June–Nov/Dec** | Strategy and spending priorities set |
| **Decision phase** | **Dec/Jan–April** | Ministers put forward **bids**; **Treasury assesses and recommends**; Budget Ministers take a preferred package to Cabinet |
| **Budget day** | **Generally May** | Parliamentary support + announcements |

**Treasury is the gate:** *"good value for money / not good value for money / pretty cool but we can't afford it."* All secret until budget day.

⭐ **Baseline vs new funding.** Baseline is committed and multi-year; changing it is *"difficult, complicated, tends to involve job losses."* **Every headline number is new money.**

**Budget 2025** (used by the exemplars): hospitals and specialist services **$5.5 bn** · cancer treatments **$1 bn** · education additional learning needs **$646 m** · maths/early intervention $100 m · school attendance $140 m · Royal Commission into abuse in care · **big year for defence**. ⚠ **Use Budget 2026 for your assignment.**

## 🌱 LSF in the budget

Treasury assesses on **value for money and strategic alignment**, and considers **the other capitals, social cohesion, wellbeing**. Agencies **outline and quantify non-financial impacts**; advice includes **trade-offs and synergies**.

**What it sounds like:** *"That initiative will save $1 billion… the trade-off will be 10,000 more children might fall into poverty, according to the agreed indicators. So you might want to offset that."*

⭐ **"Ultimately it is up to Ministers to make the decision about trade-offs."** *"There's nothing to say they have to take the advice. That is how democracy works."*

## 🔬 The policy process — six stages

| **Problem definition / opportunity** | Current settings? What's the problem? **What happens if we don't change the settings?** Who is most affected? |
| **Objectives** | What is Government trying to achieve? **How will we know? What criteria?** |
| **Options identification** | Scope? Anything off the table? ⭐ **Can we meet the objectives without regulatory change?** What do other countries do? |
| **Options analysis — cost/benefit** | Measured against our criteria; benefit **vs the status quo**; costs; funding needed? |
| **Implementation, monitoring, evaluation** | Who delivers? When? **How will we know it worked?** |
| **Advising the Minister** | Process and recommendations · **risks** · forward process |

Down the side: **"Engagement with experts and affected communities to inform advice."**

⭐ **This is the L4 business case relabelled:** problem definition = strategic case · objectives/criteria = CSFs · options identification = long list · options analysis = economic + financial case · implementation = management case · advising = preferred way forward.

⭐ **The regulation ladder:** just do it < **regulation change (hard)** < **law change (even harder)**. **Plastics** fitted inside the **Waste Minimisation Act 2008**; **fisheries** recommendations were **all within the Fisheries Act**. *"If you go outside the Act, you have to put in a whole extra saga that can take years."*

## 🎓 Where expertise enters

Officials seek expert engagement at **objectives, options identification, options analysis** — *"you might imagine in your assignments that you are feeding in here."*

⚠ **The gap:** *"Many of us who've been in a role advising government wish there was more expert advice… figuring out how to measure and monitor. But that tends to be where advice gets sought."*

**Second channel:** advising the Minister directly (slide 20's *"or maybe here"*). Contested — *"people believe this is a sacrosanct process… but both channels are valid in our democratic system, and they both happen."*

## ⚖️ Cabinet, Parliament, legislation

**Minister** sets direction and decides within portfolio. **Policy change generally requires Cabinet agreement.** **Cabinet** considers papers and makes formal decisions · proposes laws to Parliament · **approves funding**.

*"Some ministers are much more successful than others"* — swapped out for people better at convincing cabinet.

**Parliament** approves the Budget · monitors Government activity · turns policies into laws:
**IDEAS → PRE-INTRODUCTION → first reading → SELECT COMMITTEE → second reading → COMMITTEE OF THE WHOLE HOUSE → third reading → FINAL STAGES**

**Select committee** = politicians from across the spectrum *"trying to find holes… picking up counterfactuals."* In theory the law endures across governments; *"in modern democracies, a lot of that gets subverted and sidelined because the politics comes into play."*

**Politics is uncertain and messy:** contested values · contested value of evidence · contested measures · **political choices set goals for the system and drive behaviours and impact** · ⭐ **polarisation and short-term thinking is not suited to solving long-term problems**.

The Question Time clip: two people with **probably shared values** (a wealthy country where everyone eats) **talking past each other** — one framing on **the economy**, one on **child poverty**. ⭐ *"If you switched and had Marama Davidson as Prime Minister, you would have different goals and the system would respond differently."* = **L8 leverage point 3, the goals of the system.**

## 🐌 Why change is slow

**Complexity → slowness.** ⭐ *"Your view may depend on who is in charge, and whether you agree with the change."*

**The Obama/Trump test:** Obama's science advisors spent four years *"wishing they could do things faster"*, then under Trump spent two years *"thank goodness things go so slowly."*

**Immigration example (RSE / fruit pickers):** MBIE policy (problem, options, effects, costs; engagement with experts and communities) → **Minister Erica Stanford** (direction, decisions, Cabinet papers) → **Cabinet** (decides, funds, proposes laws) → **Parliament** (little law change needed) → **Immigration NZ** (operational manual, IT changes, immigration officers use new rules). Work involved a Pacific trip and ~200 growers. *"Probably didn't completely solve it, but moved it in the right direction."*

**"Never again" moments:** ⭐ *"Everyone puts down their political stripes."* **Gun law after the Christchurch mosque shooting — under three months.** The photograph shows PM, Leader of the Opposition, Deputy PM, Mayor and local MP in one frame.

⚠ **The cost of speed:** in an emergency you go *"straight from the problem to the solution, avoiding all those checks and balances."* Good decisions → great outcomes fast. Poor decisions → *"you may wish you'd gone a little bit slower."* Whakaari ~a week; weather events a week to a month; **COVID was long** — *"the longer you stay in a state of emergency, the harder it is to justify bypassing all these processes."*

> ⭐ **"Once you lose the checks and balances, you can start to threaten social cohesion."**
