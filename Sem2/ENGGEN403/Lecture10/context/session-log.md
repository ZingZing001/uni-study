### 2026-08-07 - Mode A ingest - device: macbook
Covered: full ingest of Lecture 10 (machinery of government). 33-page deck plus transcript, both good. Wrote L10-notes.md (12 concepts), L10-summary.md, L10-flashcards.tsv (80 cards, validated). Ten figures exported.

This lecture was deferred to from both L6 and L7, and it pays both debts. It exists to stop one specific sentence appearing in the projects, and Juliet says so in the first two minutes: "what we don't want is to find teams' projects that say **government should do this, government is the stakeholder**." The corrective is the best line in the lecture - "you can't just pull one lever marked government" - followed by the chain you actually have to name: which part of which ministry, advising which minister, to advocate in cabinet, for which decision, with what local consequences.

Four things carry the weight.

**Elected versus appointed (C03/C04)** is the highest-value idea, because it changes who you write for. Jacinda and Ashley looked like one unit during COVID and were structurally opposite: one elected every three years, one appointed and serving across Ardern, Hipkins and Luxon. From which follows the instruction that should shape every recommendation in the projects: "you are generally aiming at **public servants like Ashley, not at political appointments like Jacinda**."

**The policy process (C09) is the L4 business case relabelled.** Problem definition is the strategic case, objectives and criteria are the CSFs, options identification is the long list, options analysis is the economic and financial case, implementation is the management case, and advising the minister is the preferred way forward. Worth quizzing as a mapping question, because seeing it makes the assignment feel less arbitrary.

**The regulation ladder** is the most immediately useful practical test and it is transcript-only: can you just do it, or does it need a regulation change (hard), or a law change (even harder)? Both of Juliet's own reports were designed to sit inside existing legislation - plastics inside the Waste Minimisation Act 2008, fisheries inside the Fisheries Act - and L7's theme 6 was precisely the discovery that section 9c was already available and unused. **Check whether existing legislation already permits your intervention before proposing new law.**

**Baseline versus new funding (C07)** silently fixes how to read every budget number you will ever quote. Headline figures are always new money; baseline is committed, multi-year and painful to move. That makes L6's "$2 bn of new spending in a $155 bn budget" legible as a scale check rather than a curiosity.

The most transferable single anecdote is the **Obama/Trump test**: advisors who spent four years wishing government moved faster spent the next two grateful that it did not. Juliet turns it into a self-check - ask whether you would still want speed if the people in charge did not share your view. That is the honest version of "change happens slowly, and that can be good or bad."

Also captured: the Question Time clip is really an illustration of **L8 leverage point 3**. Two people with plausibly shared goals talk past each other because they have chosen different frames and measures - and "if you switched and had Marama Davidson as Prime Minister, you would have different goals and the system would respond differently."

The lecture ends deliberately pointed at L11: emergencies go "straight from the problem to the solution, avoiding all those checks and balances", and the warning to carry into Thursday is that "once you lose the checks and balances, you can start to threaten social cohesion."
