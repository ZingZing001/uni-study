Structurally this is the bookend to Lecture 2 and the toolkit for the first morning of Systems Week. Juliet takes history, VUCA and wickedness; Amanda takes the operational tools. Four things carry the weight.

**Meadows' twelve leverage points (C11)** are the most concrete deliverable in the lecture and the most likely recall item. The list runs 12 (constants, parameters, numbers) to 1 (the power to transcend paradigms), and the slide states the trade-off plainly: most interventions are designed at the shallow end because they are designable, while deeper leverage points have higher transformative impact but are harder to design for. Amanda's four worked examples ladder cleanly - an EV subsidy at 12, real-time electricity pricing at 6, rules and incentives at 5, and a non-car-oriented New Zealand within a generation at 2. Her line on the last one is the one to keep: to do it in a generation "we can't be iterative - it can't be incremental by just adding more bike lanes."

**The Head matrix (C06)** is nine named cells over two axes, and the slides give all nine. But the reason it lands is the transcript: Juliet walks one trivial problem - 800 students, a 600-seat theatre, a policy requiring a seat for everyone - through the entire matrix, from tame to very wicked, without ever changing the lecture theatre. What moves it right is adding people who disagree; what moves it up is losing clarity about the problem or the solution. That walk is transcript-only and is worth more than the diagram.

**The iceberg and the obesity build (C07, C09)** answer the question the course had left open: where problem statements actually come from. They come from the **pattern** layer. Juliet builds obesity down to "market forces dictate the food supply", picks poor nutrition as her problem statement, and explicitly parks limited activity - "I don't think I can manage to put both of those in my problem statement... it might be that you need two." That is the direct bridge into L4's two-to-three problem statements. She also makes a point of showing two models in parallel to demonstrate there is no right answer, and volunteers that she originally drew one of them upside down.

**The school-discipline causal loop (C12)** is the sharpest single artefact in the lecture. Sending disruptive students to the principal reduces disruption for everyone else - a balancing loop that achieves its goal. The same suspension puts those students out of class, so they fall behind, focus less, and act out more - a reinforcing loop. The intervention that balances one loop drives another. That is the wicked-problem definition in miniature, and it is a better illustration of "amelioration measures may create new problems" than the definition itself.
