Covered: full ingest of Lecture 12 (City Rail Link guest lecture, Simon Mace and Simon Fenton). **Transcript only - no deck.** Wrote L12-notes.md (11 concepts), L12-summary.md, L12-flashcards.tsv (75 cards, validated). No figures, because there is nothing to rasterise.

This lecture closes a loop the course opened in week two. L4's very first example of a systems-level business case was City Rail Link, and this is the same project narrated by the people delivering it, 30 days before opening.

Two halves, and the second is the more valuable one.

**Simon Mace's half** is the project and the business case. The single best moment is that he **went back to the 2015 business case and checked it**: "the same objectives that we are delivering now. So that's reassuring." Eleven years, a change of cost base and a pandemic later, the strategic case held. That is a real, empirical answer to L4-C13's warning that a business case is only a cross-section in time - it says **the costs decay, the case for change may not**. He also supplies a term L5 never named: **wider economic benefits**, the agglomeration effects of connecting labour markets.

**Simon Fenton's half is the one worth studying.** Its premise - *"consultancy engineering doesn't end with a drawing"* - fills a genuine hole in the course, which has taught business cases, options assessment and stakeholder analysis but never what happens after approval. Four things stand out.

**The producer statement chain (C07)** is concrete and examinable: PS1 design, PS2 independent check, PS3 built as drawn, PS4 CPS observation, plus a parallel DC1-DC4 chain for client requirements. And the distinction between **hold points** (mandatory, because there is a point of no return) and **witness points** (discretionary, and explicitly **flexed according to how well the constructor is performing**) is a nice piece of control theory hiding in a quality process.

**"Explain the why, not just the what" (C09)** is the most portable idea in the lecture. The box-out example is perfect: a bare "no" makes the contractor come back; explaining that the bars are carrying tensile loads lets them design their own alternative. And his framing that **communication is a hard skill, not a soft one** - because it can be learned, and because most project failures are relational rather than technical - is worth carrying into Systems Week.

**Engineering judgement (C10)**, formalised as *experience + evidence + common sense*, with a worked example that takes about a minute: a dented panel needs scaffold, puts someone over a 20-metre drop, costs money, risks collateral damage, and the station opens next week - so the case for fixing it evaporates. That is L4-C12's "no right answer, only trade-offs" compressed to a site decision.

**The paper pyramid (C11)** ends with the line that justifies every boring assurance process in the course: **"You can't make it up. You can't find photos that were never taken."** 25,630 as-built drawings, 20,000 client requirements, 109 PS4 sets, 74 building consents, all funnelling into practical completion on 30 June.

Also worth keeping: BIM at LOD 500 was used as a **stakeholder management tool**, not just a design tool - shown to mana whenua groups and "I lost count of how many politicians" to win buy-in. That is L11's visualisation argument arriving from a completely different direction.
