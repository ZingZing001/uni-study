### 2026-08-07 - Mode A ingest - device: macbook
Covered: full ingest of Lecture 11 (guest lecture, Tom Wilson, NEMA). 37-page deck plus transcript, both good. Wrote L11-notes.md (12 concepts), L11-summary.md, L11-flashcards.tsv (90 cards, validated). Twelve figures exported.

This is the best-constructed lecture in the course so far, and it is not really about emergencies. It is about **getting technical knowledge into a decision under time pressure**, which is the thing every other lecture has circled. Tom says so directly: "I've used the term science advisers, but I'm hoping you guys will identify with that role as being **future technical experts operating in deeply complex systems**."

Four things carry the weight.

**The bread analogy (C08)** is the single most useful artefact. Data is wheat, analysis makes a loaf, operational context is the chef, and the same evidence has to come out as a sub, toast or gluten-free depending on whether it is going to public messaging, contingency planning or a governance decision. The red text on the slide is the diagnosis: "**often emergency managers are required to do both**" and "**with little time or expertise, even the best data doesn't get the opportunity to add value**." That is L3's gangs report and L7's fisheries data problem stated as a general mechanism - **the failure is never the science, it is the translation**.

**Communicating risk (C07)** contains two moves worth stealing outright. First, the evacuation table: 70% compliance gives 22,180 deaths, 0% gives 68,670 - injuries rise 23% but deaths rise 210%, and that asymmetry *is* the argument for evacuation readiness without a word of rhetoric. Second, the probability reframing: a 1% chance in 50 years is ignorable, so he recomputed it as **12% in three years, 23% in six** - the length of one and two electoral terms. Same data, rescaled to the decision-maker's horizon. That is a directly reusable technique for the projects.

**Taupo (C09)** is the best worked example of advice under pressure anywhere in the course. The geology was the easy part; the hard part was that it came two or three years after Whakaari, with the same government, with GNS under judicial proceedings over its Whakaari advice, and with social licence fraying post-COVID. His response was to commission the science agency to change how it communicated - **from prediction to a scenario table**, six graded outcomes each anchored to a real historical eruption, with **two independent likelihood estimates published side by side even though they disagree**. And the success test is a line worth remembering: Gary Knowles said "that looks really simple, no dramas", and Tom concluded they were on the right track. **Advice succeeds when the decision-maker can act, not when it is complete.**

**The science/policy boundary (C10)** is the theory that explains the other three. Policy needs relevance, science needs credibility, and the hybrid zone needs **legitimacy**, manufactured through balance, compromise, inclusion and transparency. It explains why good scientists give bad advice and good officials misread good science - they are optimising different variables and neither is wrong.

Two structural things worth noting. The **97% figure** - the share of central government spend going to response and recovery rather than reduction - is the cleanest one-number argument for risk reduction in the whole course, and it connects to L6's budget material. And the **states of emergency** answer is a nice systems-thinking trap: the count is rising partly because the hazard is rising, but also because **declaring is a tool** that unlocks resources and creates a focusing effect in communities. The metric measures the response, not just the world.
