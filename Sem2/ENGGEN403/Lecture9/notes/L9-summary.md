# ⚡ ENGGEN403 L9 — one-page summary

## 🧭 Understand before you solve

*"This is the first thing you'll do in Systems Week — **spending the first couple of days really understanding that problem**. We have to understand the problem before we can even think about coming to solutions."*

⚠ **The named failure:** teams reach problem statements by end of day 1, hit the **solution phase Monday evening**, start the **financial analysis**, and by **Tuesday night / Wednesday morning** realise it isn't a problem they can affect — then go **all the way back to the beginning**.

**Iteration itself is fine** — *"you are going to have to iterate, you are going to have to recycle. But make sure you understand the system before you start that."*

> **"There are no solutions to complex challenges — only an endless series of adaptive responses."** — David Gurteen

## 🔀 Diverge / converge, twice

**DISCOVER → DEFINE → DEVELOP → DELIVER** (double diamond, four stages here). Problem space: diverge on causes → converge on **one** problem statement. Solution space: diverge on options → converge on a shortlist.

**5 whys → nodes; causal loop → connections.** *"Complements systems thinking by revealing the variables that drive system behaviour… we'll build on this by connecting those variables into a causal loop diagram."*

## 🕸 Mind map vs causal loop

Mind maps are good at listing variables. They cannot show **interconnectedness** — *"if I increase something on one side, what happens on the other side?"*

> ⭐ **A mind map is an inventory. A causal loop is a model.**

(The deck's mind map is captioned *"Generated using AI"* — fine for the divergent inventory, not for the relational modelling.)

## ➰ Causal loop legend

- **Variables (nodes)** = key factors · **arrows** = cause and effect
- **+** = variables move the **same direction** · **−** = **opposite directions**
- **Reinforcing (R)** = amplifies change · **Balancing (B)** = counteracts change
- **Leverage points** = where interventions have greatest impact
- **Problem statements** summarise what the diagram reveals

⭐ **Name nodes neutrally:** *"bioplastics"*, **not** *"increasing bioplastics"* — the arrow carries the direction.
⚠ **Name nodes specifically:** *"If you say waste — production waste? retail waste? If you say utilities — in general, or specifically fuel?"* You cannot sign an arrow between two fuzzy nodes.

## ➕➖ Signs and parity ⭐

> **"It doesn't refer to whether it is increasing or decreasing. It refers to the POLARITY — the direction of change between the two nodes."**

A **+** arrow can describe two variables **falling together**.

| **Even** number of **−** (including **zero**) | **Reinforcing (R)** |
| **Odd** number of **−** | **Balancing (B)** |

*"If you have two negatives, it flips you one way and flips you back."* ⚠ **Not on any slide** — transcript only.

**Gym example:** McDonald's every night, no gym → reinforcing. Add the gym → balancing back to equilibrium.

## 🔢 The five steps

1 **Identify variables** · 2 **determine relationships** · 3 **identify feedback loops that explain why the problem persists** · 4 **identify leverage points** · 5 **develop a systems problem statement** describing the problem, who is affected, why it exists, and its consequences.

*"All we're doing is **mapping the system behaviour**."* The intuition comes from doing it — *"it's not going to just spit out at you what your problem statement should be."*

## 🎚 Leverage points

> **"A place in a system where a small change can create a disproportionately large change throughout the entire system."**

**Look for variables that:** are **highly connected**, influencing multiple loops · **affect many other variables** · ⭐ **can be realistically changed through an intervention**.

⭐ **Proximity trade-off:** *"The closer the leverage point is to the system itself, the smaller the impact — but the easier it is to design and implement."*

⭐ **The lake / Band-Aid test:** *"We can treat the lake — but that's ultimately not going to matter if we don't stop the pollution going into the lake."* **Is my intervention upstream or downstream of what regenerates the problem?**

## 📝 The problem statement template ⭐

> **The problem is [briefly describe the problem], which affects [who is impacted], because [why the problem exists], leading to [what negative consequences exist].**

**Four parts: problem · who · why · consequences.**

**Worked (plastics):** *"New Zealand's plastic waste management infrastructure is underdeveloped, affecting communities and the environment, because limited recycling capacity reduces plastic recovery, leading to increased environmental pollution and long-term ecosystem damage."*

**vs How Might We** (303, B2C — intended action · potential user · desired outcome): HMW frames an **opportunity**; the systems statement is a **diagnosis** and contains **why the problem persists**. Some teams still start from HMW to simplify.

## 🍞 The 2024 food-prices exemplar

⚠ **The annotated slide is missing from the deck** (footers 17–22 absent) — reconstructed from speech.

**PS1 — retail competition:** *"It doesn't matter what we do in the production space if the supermarkets are always just going to charge a high price anyway. If there's no competition, there's no incentive to lower the prices."*
> *"High grocery prices affect New Zealand consumers because limited market competition reduces downward pressure on prices, leading to higher living costs."*

**PS2 — transport and logistics:** *"You can't do anything if your transport system is going to be inefficient anyway."*

⭐ **Both arguments have the same shape:** *whatever else you fix, this bottleneck re-imposes the problem.* That's the Band-Aid test used to choose between leverage points.

**Student loops from the live activity:**
- **Geography** — NZ far away → logistics → hard to export → **+** food price; low population density → **−** economies of scale → less automation → **+** price. **Reinforcing.**
- **Congestion** → **+** travel distance → **+** fuel consumption → **+** fuel cost → **+** food cost. **Reinforcing.**
- **Demand collapse** — fuel → **+** taxes → **+** utilities → **+** labour cost → **−** food purchasing → **−** competition → job loss → **−** labour cost. **Balancing** (odd number of minuses — classified by the rule, not by intuition).

## 🎯 One statement, one leverage point ⭐

> *"**You can't address everything in the system with one problem statement.** A common issue teams had last year is that they were trying to address **too much of the system in a single problem statement**… you're addressing **one either loop or one leverage point**. **You're never going to get anywhere** otherwise."*

Same instruction as L8-C09 (Juliet parked *limited activity* to keep *poor nutrition*). L4 expects **2–3 problem statements**, so parked material has somewhere to go.

## 🧰 Problem framing canvas — if stuck

> *"If I had an hour to solve a problem I'd spend 55 minutes thinking about the problem and five minutes thinking about solutions."* — Einstein

Panels: **first-cut problem statement** (whose problem, what need, why a problem) · **is there a problem behind the problem?** (5 whys) · **How Might We…?** · ⭐ **what type of problem is this?** (**Cynefin**: complex / complicated / chaotic / clear) · **rich picture** · **frame the problem three different ways** (iceberg, etc.) · **who cares enough to act?** (salience + power/interest, L7) · ⭐ ***"does anyone benefit from the problem as a problem?"*** · **any changes to your first-cut statement?**

The two genuinely new items are **Cynefin** and the **vested-interest question** — no other tool in the course asks the latter.

**Clinic:** *"When you're crafting your problem statements, come see the clinic. We give lots and lots of feedback on those statements."*
