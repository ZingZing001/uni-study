# ENGGEN403 L1 — one-page summary

**Real vs assessment problems.** Course problems arrive framed, sized, and guaranteed solvable. Real ones don't: framing is part of the work and some have no solution. Examples: unemployment, AI reliability, energy demand — interconnected (AI/data centres → energy demand → prices → cost of living → unemployment). Engineering alone won't close them.

**T-shaped engineer.** Vertical = depth in ≥1 discipline. Horizontal = breadth about innovation enabling effective work with other disciplines "to bring their ideas to life." Both required; breadth without depth is not a T. Richard Miller (US NAE): fundamentals "important, but not enough"; Grand Challenges "won't be solved by engineers alone"; **"doubling down on even more hard sciences and math will not help"** — need skillset *and* mindset. Embedded in professional degrees globally; via Engineering NZ / **Washington Accord**, part of professional sign-off.

**Fixed vs growth mindset** (Dweck). A **continuum**, not two types, and **per-skill**. Fixed: ability innate, unchangeable. Growth: ability improvable through practice.

| | Fixed | Growth |
|---|---|---|
| Failure | permanent | chance to learn / pivot |
| Critical feedback | personal attack | chance to improve, build new systems |
| Tasks | easier ones, minimal effort | challenging ones, work hard |
| Obstacles | give up | experiment, problem-solve |
| Focus | measurable accomplishments | journey of continual improvement |
| Creative risk | avoided | route to innovate |

Fixed logic is self-consistent — "if talent is fixed, why bother?" **Biological basis:** neuroplasticity; new pathways form from learning new things, "especially as you get older"; same mechanism as stroke recovery, running in healthy brains constantly. **Limits are real:** physical ceilings exist (the figure-skating failure); most assumed ceilings aren't (the public-speaking success).

**Grit** (Duckworth). = **passion and perseverance for your goals, over a sustained period of time.** Time clause is what separates it from motivation.
Four pillars: **1 passion/interest** (can't be determined about what doesn't interest you) · **2 deliberate practice** (won't work first time) · **3 sense of purpose** (carries the days you don't enjoy) · **4 hope/optimism** (for when it looks unfinishable).
Consequences: perseverance/resilience/mental toughness/determination → achieving life goals; high grit → better at finding solutions when problems arise.
**Talent ≠ grit**, "maybe inversely related" — talented people found things easy, never learned to fail, never built perseverance tools. Grit is trainable.
**Hope curve:** Systems Week teams start excited, despair creeps in ~Wednesday afternoon — that's where hope gets tested.

**Delayed gratification.** Ability to delay instant gratification in favour of long-term success. Marshmallow test (4-year-old, wait and get two), linked in lecture to the Dunedin study, reported as "one of the best predictors of overall career success." Measures impulse control / willingness to strive for a bigger goal. Self-given caveat: also predicts not liking marshmallows. *⚠ Attribution is shaky — marshmallow test is Mischel's, not originally Dunedin. Use the slide's plain claim, not the study name.*

**Failure as information.** Not a verdict — a diagnosis. Sequence that matters: failure → identify the specific weak spot → targeted effort. (Lectureship: failed 3×, got it on the 4th.) **Earlier failure is better** — first failure at 45–55 is much harder to absorb than an early one bounced back from.

**Discomfort zone.** Too easy → complete it, learn nothing. Too hard → can't do it, learn nothing. Between → growth. "Just hard enough but not too hard." Discomfort is the *signal*, not the cost — the stated design rationale for Systems Week. Student phrasing: growth happens in the discomfort zone "when we take small but deliberate actions to change ingrained habits."

**SMART(H) goals.** Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound, + proposed **H = Hard**. Note the deliberate tension: *Achievable* invites aiming low; the entire lecture argues for resolving it toward Hard, since a goal you're confident of meeting sits in the "too easy" band.

**Coherence beats cleverness.** "A solution only gains credibility when the strategic case, options analysis, CBA metrics, and rollout plan tell the same story; otherwise decision-makers fixate on inconsistencies." Coherence across parts > brilliance in any one part. Corollary: "Evidence is a team sport." Communication style is an *input* to system success — one student's style created a negative feedback loop. Brainiest 30 people who don't talk = bad result.

**Learning outcomes as stated:** describe and apply growth mindset; describe and apply grit; consider how both apply to your own life and aspirations; identify areas where you hold a fixed mindset.
