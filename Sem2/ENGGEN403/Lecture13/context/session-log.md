Four things are new and worth the ingest.

**Systems research vs scholarly research (C08)** is a named learning outcome and appears nowhere else in the course. Five clean contrasts: hypothesis vs forecast oriented, scientifically validated methodology vs valid information and analysis, past literature vs literature plus projections, advances-in-the-field vs decision-making, publications vs shared internally with the client. The compression that makes it stick: **systems research asks how the world is arranged; scholarly research asks what is true.** And the practical version is concrete - how is this regulated, what industries are impacted, how many jobs, what exports.

**Team structure and the too-much-resource problem (C04)** inverts the assumption you would carry in from every previous group project. At 35 people the failure mode is not free-riding, it is **under-utilisation** - "I never thought I would be in a situation where I had too much resource." Four structures are shown and none is endorsed; the instruction is to settle it on the Thursday before the brief lands.

**Doing vs managing, and trust at scale (C05, C06)** is the most uncomfortable and most useful part. Managing a large group is itself a substantial body of work, and the failure story is a team leader who rewrote everything and stayed up all night. Then the instruction that will feel wrong: a subset of your team will define the problem, you may not get input, and **"you pretty much run from what you're given... you have to take as a given that your teammates have done good work."** With **lockdown points** as the mechanism that makes sequential handoffs close. At this scale **trust replaces consensus** - which is a real professional skill being taught deliberately.

**Why systems work, and who for (C11)** is the sharpest reframe in the lecture and it retroactively improves L9. "Don't walk into the examination of a system saying it's a mess and broken for everyone. **It's probably only broken for some of its stakeholders.**" The hospital ED example lands it - the system has accumulated over time and some stakeholder groups are happy with the outcomes, the patients not among them. That is the general answer to L9's framing-canvas question *does anyone benefit from the problem as a problem*, and it explains why interventions get resisted by forces you never mapped.

Also captured: **"intervention", not "solution"** as the correct vocabulary for a wicked problem; **packages** as the thing 303 never had; and the **deliverable structure** - executive summary, strategic case, economic case, appendix, references, about twelve pages, **with no financial or management case**. That last point is worth confirming against the real brief.
