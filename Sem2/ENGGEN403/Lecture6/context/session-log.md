### 2026-08-07 - Mode A ingest - device: macbook
Covered: full ingest of Lecture 6 (GDP, government spending, and what metrics miss). 35-page deck plus transcript, both good. Wrote L6-notes.md (13 concepts), L6-summary.md, L6-flashcards.tsv (126 cards, validated). Eleven figures exported.

First co-delivered lecture: **Marc Lewis** on GDP, productivity and the worked examples, **Juliet Gerrard** on the limitations and the wellbeing frameworks. The split matters, because the two halves are in tension by design and Juliet says so outright: *"We are giving you assignments on wicked problems that can't be solved, and the tools that we've given you can't solve them."* This lecture is the course admitting the limits of the toolkit it spent the week teaching.

The four things that carry weight:

**The four capitals (C12).** Juliet's explicit steer - *"this is the place I suggest you concentrate"* - and the slide gives full definitions of each, which the lecture never read aloud. Natural, human, social, financial and physical, framed as **assets that generate wellbeing now and into the future**. The weighting between them is stated to be a **values choice for the team**, which is exactly the kind of instruction that gets tested by asking you to defend a weighting.

**Multi-criteria analysis (C13).** The tool you actually apply. The NZTA magnitude scale runs +3 to -3, and the tiers separate on **duration, reversibility and mitigability** - not on how bad something feels. Then the same rule as L4 and L5, stated for a third time in one week and more bluntly: *"If that option is a 16 for the natural capital, but this one is a 17, and we chose it because it's got 17 - I'll put a big fat zero through your discussion."* Three lecturers-worth of repetition on one point is not subtle.

**The negatives gap.** Marc's marking observation, which is the single most actionable thing in the lecture: *"Students have been great at describing what the positive impacts are. It's a little bit weaker on describing the negative impacts, or what those trade-offs are."* The plastics table proves the point - the do-maximum package's natural-capital column includes *"recycling and composting facilities can have high energy and water usage"*. An environmental intervention with an environmental cost. Every option hits every capital both ways.

**The scale check (C07).** Revenues ~$145 bn, expenditures ~$155 bn, and **new spending only ~$2 bn**. If a systems project proposes a $150 bn solution, it has proposed the entire government. This is a cheap sanity test and easy to fail.

Two things worth holding for their own sake. Juliet's **stocks-versus-flows** explanation of GDP is the clearest teaching in the lecture - buying ten pairs of shoes counts, owning fifty doesn't, giving them away doesn't; and two 70-year-olds, one with nothing and one with plenty, look identical in GDP. Scale that up and refurbished hospitals and mended potholes are invisible. And the **productivity story**: NZ produces $93.56/hour against Australia's $121.38 for six *fewer* hours a week, and the cause is what we produce, not how hard we work - in the 1970s we matched Scandinavia, then they went into pharmaceuticals and software and we stayed in forestry and dairy.

He Ara Waiora is on the deck in full detail (ends, means, all five principles) but Juliet explicitly recommends against using it for the assignment - less developed, fewer exemplars, no government-website instructions - while noting it carries the one thing the Western frameworks lack: **intergenerational prosperity**. Captured in full anyway, since one exemplar used it as an extra MCA row.
