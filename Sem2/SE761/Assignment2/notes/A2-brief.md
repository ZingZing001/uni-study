## 🎯 What it actually asks

> [!quote] Learning outcomes (brief, p.1)
> - **Demonstrate an understanding of Agile values and principles, and the Scrum framework**
> - **Predict and assess the impact of Agile and Scrum aspects that will be problematic in your project**
> - **Relate previous software development experience** to the anticipated Agile and Scrum practices

The core question, in one sentence:

> **How will Agile values and the Scrum framework actually play out for you, given that this is a university course and not a full-time industry job — and what does your own past experience predict about that?**

> [!important] You do **not** need your project allocated
> *"You should be able to complete this assignment without any of the specifics of whatever project that will eventually be allocated to you."* The focus is **the anticipated Scrum framework and how the prescribed Scrum Guide steps will be played out** — not your project's domain.
>
> (You do now have a project, per [[../../Lecture5/notes/L5-notes|L5]]. It's still not required, and specifics risk crowding out the reflection.)

## ⚠️ The four traps, named in the brief

> [!danger] 1. Writing "a series of pros and cons" in general terms
> > *"If the report reads as a series of 'pros and cons', speaking in general terms, etc., it will come across as being written by ChatGPT. **That's not a reflection, and you might be asked to redo the assignment to be more reflective.**"*
>
> This is the only place in either brief that threatens a **redo**. The test: could a classmate who has never met you have written this sentence? If yes, it isn't reflection.

> [!danger] 2. Using ChatGPT for content — the reversal from A1
> A1 **encouraged** it and gave **2 marks** for a declared reflection on use. **A2 says you shouldn't use it for creating your content**, because *"ChatGPT doesn't know your personal history and past experiences — therefore it [can't] reflect on your behalf."*
>
> ⚠ **Unlike A1 and A3, A2 has no Gen AI declaration section** — there is no mechanism for disclosing use, because the brief's position is simply don't. See [[../../Assignment1/notes/A1-brief|A1]] for the contrast — **do not carry the A1 habit over.**

> [!danger] 3. Repeating definitions
> > *"The reader needs to be convinced that you have a deep understanding, and that **you aren't merely repeating definitions of Agile and Scrum**."*
>
> Definitions are the price of entry, not the content. Every definition you spend words on should immediately be followed by *"which in our case will mean…"*.

> [!danger] 4. The word limit, both directions
> **800–900 words**, and **penalties apply for going over**. But also: *"If your content is noticeably empty, this won't appear like you maximised the space available."*
>
> The lecturer's advice for the identically-limited A3 ([[../../Lecture6/notes/L6-notes|L6]]) applies: **target 800**, drift a little over, **never past 900**.

> [!tip] What *is* allowed
> **Personal language is fine** — *"but this doesn't mean you can be slack and casual. You still need to be professional in your tone."* Note this is the exact opposite of A1, which banned personal language.
>
> **Write in full paragraphs** (A1 was bullet points).

## 🔄 Choosing a reflection model

[[../../Lecture5/notes/L5-notes|L5]] covered four formal models (`Lecture5/resources/Models-of-reflection.pdf`). The lecturer's position:

> *"You need not do that… we are not going to mark you against these models, but here is some knowledge that will definitely help you in writing your reflections."*
>
> And: *"If you do [follow one], then do refer to [it] in your submission — that I did follow this specific reflection [model]."*

> [!important] ⭐ Gibbs' **Analysis** phase *is* the A2 question
> The lecturer said this outright: *"That analysis part is what is being asked in assignment two."*
>
> Gibbs' Analysis cue questions:
> - **"What sense can you make of the situation?"**
> - ⭐ **"How do past experiences compare to this?"** ← the 3-mark rubric line, verbatim
> - **"How does theory or evidence fit with this?"** ← Agile values and Scrum are the theory
> - **"How did your involvement affect the outcome?"**
>
> If you want a spine for the report, that's it — and it maps onto the rubric almost line for line.

| Model | Shape | Best for A2 if… |
|---|---|---|
| **Gibbs' reflective cycle** | Description → Feelings → Evaluation → **Analysis** → Conclusion → Action plan | …you want the most direct fit. The Analysis phase is literally the rubric |
| **Johns' model of structured reflection** | Describe → Reflect → Influencing factors → Could I have dealt with it better? → Learning | …your past experience turned on a **decision** you'd now make differently. Forces "look inwards" (your thoughts/feelings) **and** "look outwards" (the situation, your actions, whether they were ethical, external factors) |
| **Kolb's learning cycle** | Concrete experience → Reflective observation → **Abstract conceptualisation** → Active experimentation | …you have **more than one** past project and want to **draw generalisations** across them. That generalisation step is what distinguishes Kolb |
| **Rolfe, Freshwater & Jasper** | **What? → So what? → Now what?** | …you're short on time. Three questions, and *"So what?"* is already the vocabulary from [[../../Lecture2/notes/L2-notes|L2-C02]] |

> [!tip] The lecturer explicitly permits a hybrid
> *"You can also pick a few things from this model, a few things from that model, and then apply them as a unique or custom model."*

### Kanban / XP / other (1 mark)
- **XP** — [[../../Lecture5/notes/L5-notes|L5]] gives you the definition to use: *"whatever good practices are implemented in traditional development, we use them in an **extreme** manner"* — code review every sprint becomes **pair programming**, i.e. review continuously. Also TDD, continuous integration, refactoring.
- **Kanban** — named in [[../../Lecture3/notes/L3-notes|L3]]; origin covered in [[../../Lecture5/notes/L5-notes|L5]] (Toyota, 1950s; adapted to software by **David Anderson**).
- **Scrumban** — named in L3's terminology list.
- ⚠ **Open question from [[../../Lecture4/notes/L4-notes|L4]]:** whether "one other practice" must be Kanban/XP specifically, or whether any named agile practice qualifies. The brief says *"Kanban, Extreme Programming (XP), or other principles"* — **"or other" is in the brief**, so a named, justified alternative should be safe.
- The brief accepts a **negative** answer here: *"If not, what ones did you consider but opted not to incorporate?"* A well-argued rejection scores the same as an adoption.

### Past experience + counterfactual (3 marks) — the block only you can write
[[../../Lecture5/notes/L5-notes|L5]] lowered the bar considerably:

> *"It is **not important that you name a specific process model** that you used in your previous experience. As long as you know that it was different from Agile… If you have been part of a software project development team — whether a student team, or industry, or maybe freelance work — you may bring that experience into this assignment."*

> [!tip] If you genuinely have nothing
> [[../../Lecture4/notes/L4-notes|L4]]'s brief offered a fallback: reflect on a project from the Part IV catalogue (`Lecture2/resources/project_pages/`). Weaker, but permitted.

> [!important] The counterfactual is the assessed part
> The rubric doesn't ask what happened. It asks you to *"compare them meaningfully with **how the outcome might have changed if an Agile approach was followed**."*
>
> So each past-experience paragraph needs three moves:
> 1. **What happened**, concretely — a specific project, a specific failure or success
> 2. **The mechanism** — *why* it happened, in Agile/Scrum vocabulary
> 3. **The counterfactual** — which specific practice would have caught it, and honestly, whether it actually would have

> [!example] The richest raw material you have is [[../../Lecture4/notes/L4-notes|L4]]
> The paper-plane simulation is a **shared, documented experience where the class knew the Scrum rules and broke them anyway under time pressure** — a forgotten requirement, an untested target, four competing designs in a leaderless team. Your L4 notes already map each failure to the L3 concept it violated.
>
> Two cautions: it is very recent (the "previous experience" framing fits a real project better), and **everyone in the class has it**, which makes it the least distinctive thing you could write about. Best used as **corroboration** for a pattern you first saw in your own project, not as the main exhibit.
